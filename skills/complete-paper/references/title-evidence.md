# Title: what the evidence does and does not support

Read this when you need to explain a choice made in `parts/title.md`. It was written and checked against the original sources for the stand-alone `paper-title` skill (2026-11-19) and is carried over unchanged; verify each entry before citing it elsewhere.

Use this to explain choices honestly. Do not tell the user that a title will raise citations or AI visibility; no study below supports a promise like that. The defensible claims are narrower: the result can be found with standard terms, the title survives title-only triage, and nothing in it overclaims.

- **Length: mixed.** Among each year's 20,000 most-cited papers (2007-2013), shorter titles went with more citations, but within journals the effect held only for 2007-2010 and the authors claim no causation (Letchford et al. 2015, R. Soc. Open Sci.). Across 4.3 million papers, short titles went with more citations only among the most-cited papers, and for typical papers the correlation was usually negative (Sienkiewicz & Altmann 2016, R. Soc. Open Sci.). Shorter titles went with more views and citations in 423 PLoS and BMC articles (Paiva et al. 2012, Clinics); length had no effect in 2,439 ecology and evolution papers (Heard et al. 2023, FACETS).
- **Stating the result: weakly supported.** Results-describing titles were cited more than methods-describing ones (Paiva et al. 2012).
- **Acronyms: negative.** Titles with acronyms were cited less (Heard et al. 2023), and APS style discourages nonstandard ones.
- **Question marks and colons: contradictory.** Negative in Jamali & Nikzad 2011 (Scientometrics; 2,172 PLoS articles: question titles downloaded more but cited less, colon titles lower on both) and in Paiva et al. 2012; slightly positive in Heard et al. 2023. The rules above rest on triage logic, venue convention and house preference, not on citation data.
- **Full-sentence titles: slightly positive** in Heard et al. 2023, and still excluded here by house rule; the result goes into the noun phrase instead.
- **Humour: not harmful to citation by people** once paper importance is controlled (Heard et al. 2023). The standalone-hook rule is about search and triage only.
- **Title and abstract are embedded together** by paper-embedding models such as SPECTER2. Which retriever a given agent uses varies, so the abstract patch is cheap insurance. (The generalization is an inference.)
- **LLM recall without retrieval** leans toward highly cited, recent, shorter-titled, fewer-author papers: about 275,000 references generated by GPT-4o for 10,000 papers (Algaba et al. 2025, arXiv:2504.02767). Correlational; not a reason to shorten.
- **Prestige metadata moves LLM recommendations.** With title and abstract fixed, swapping venue, h-index, citation-count and affiliation metadata changed 39.2% of recommendations pooled over eight LLMs (Jinadu et al., EMNLP 2026, arXiv:2609.00248). Titles were never varied, so this shows that prestige matters, not that wording does not.
- **Agent citations vary from run to run.** One small test (three deep-research tools, three runs each, one prompt) found an average pairwise Jaccard overlap of reference lists of 31% for the most consistent tool (arXiv:2509.11295). Indicative only.
- **Some agents read open-access text only**; Weidener et al. 2026 (arXiv:2601.12542) call this a fundamental constraint of their system.
- **Title changes break version linking.** Title matching cannot connect a preprint to a published version whose title changed, and about 11% of sampled arXiv computer-science preprints were published under a different title (Lin et al. 2020, Scientometrics).
- **"Generative engine optimization" phrasing tricks** have mostly been tested on documents that were already retrieved, which says little about being found (Martinez 2026, arXiv:2607.14035).
