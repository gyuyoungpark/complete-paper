# Prose: sources and this skill's own rules

Read this when you need to explain a choice made in `parts/prose.md`. There is good evidence that the vocabulary of scientific papers shifted when language models came into use, and a well-kept catalogue of the patterns such models produce. There is no evidence that removing those patterns makes a paper better, and none is claimed: the rules of the part rest on what costs a reader attention.

Checked 2026-11-23. Verify each entry against the current page before citing it elsewhere.

## Sources on machine tone

- **Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing"** (an advice page of WikiProject AI Cleanup; not a policy). A catalogue of wording and formatting patterns seen in text generated by chatbots, with examples taken from Wikipedia articles and drafts. Among its groups: undue emphasis on significance, legacy and broader trends; promotional language; editorial commentary; habits of style and formatting. The page states that it is not a ban on words or punctuation, that the patterns are only potential signs of a problem, and that treating the signs as the thing to fix could merely make detection harder while the underlying problems stay. It also notes that some signs apply only in the context of Wikipedia, and that models differ from one another. Read here through search excerpts and news reports of 2025; the page itself changes often.
- **Kobak, González-Márquez, Horvát and Lause, "Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary", Sci. Adv. 11, eadt3813 (2025).** Vocabulary of more than 15 million PubMed abstracts from 2010 to 2024: the arrival of language models was followed by an abrupt rise in the frequency of certain words, most of them words of style and not of content. The authors publish their list of excess words with the analysis code. A later preprint from the same group (arXiv:2608.10715) applies the method to full texts; it was seen here only as a search excerpt.
- **What follows for this skill.** The watch-lists of `prose_lint.py` are this skill's own selection, informed by these two sources; they are not copies of either list and have not been validated against one.

## Journal rules used in the part

- **APS Journals Style Guide (February 2026).** An acronym used only once is spelled out; an abbreviation used more than once is defined at first use, and again in the text even if the abstract defined it; one definition throughout, and no excess of acronyms. Numbers up to ten are spelled out in running text, and a sentence does not begin with a numeral. "E.g." and "i.e." are not interchangeable; "cf." means "compare".
- **Nature, formatting guide.** The summary paragraph avoids numbers and abbreviations unless essential.

## Sources on readable prose (from memory; not re-read here)

- **Gopen and Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", American Scientist 78, 550 (1990)**: readers expect the subject and the verb close together, familiar information at the start of a sentence and new information at its end. The rules "old before new" and "actor as subject" follow it.
- **Williams, "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"**: characters as subjects, actions as verbs; the source of the rule against nominalization.

## What was measured here (2026-11-23)

- `scripts/prose_lint.py`: 9 planted-fault cases pass (`evals/text/cases.py`), among them a paragraph on which every category fires and two comparisons of an edit.
- The example manuscript of `examples/supplement/`: 43 sentences, mean 14.9 words, none over 30, no hit in any category (remeasured 2026-11-24, after its colons and semicolons were removed).
- Two manuscripts from arXiv (cond-mat.mes-hall, 2026):

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Experimental article with supporting information | 301 | 25.8 | 29% | long (35), opener (30), intensifier (13) |
| Letter with theory supplement | 249 | 17.7 | 10% | opener (9), long (8) |

  - A true finding on the first: a doubled word ("are are"). A false one, since removed: "10 10" from a product of numbers counted as a doubled word. Author and affiliation lines were counted as sentences until the text before the first heading was left out.
  - Few hits of "inflated" or "hype" (two and three in 7777 words): for these two papers the lint was mostly a check of sentence length.
- The lint has not been run on text known to be drafted by a language model, so nothing is measured about how well the lists catch it.

## Cautions

- No pattern is proof of how a text was written, and this skill does not offer detection.
- Detectors of AI writing are not addressed; `parts/prose.md` promises nothing about them.
- Lists age. A word that is typical of one model and year is not typical of the next.

## This skill's own rules (no external source)

- The premise of the part: machine tone is a symptom, and the repair is to ask what the sentence claims.
- The edit guard: an edit for style changes no number, citation, label, formula or strength of claim, checked by `prose_lint.py --compare`.
- The limit of 40 words per sentence in the body, and "most under 25". The limit of 30 for the abstract and the conclusion comes from those parts.
- Abbreviations kept only when used at least three times. APS asks only that an acronym used once be spelled out; three is this skill's threshold.
- The list of usages common in drafts by writers of English as a second language. It is a list of this skill's author's experience, not the result of a corpus study.
- "Change the fewest words that fix the problem", and leaving a correct sentence in the author's manner alone.
- That editing the tone of a text does not change the duty to disclose the use of an AI tool.
