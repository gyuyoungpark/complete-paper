# Conclusion: journal rules, advice and studies

Read this when you need to explain a choice made in `parts/conclusion.md`. Journals say little about conclusions, and what they say conflicts: some require a summary, some remove one. The structure in the part combines genre research with editors' advice. None of it is a controlled test of what readers retain.

Checked 2026-11-19. Verify each entry against the current page before citing it elsewhere.

## What journals require

- **Physical Review Letters.** A Letter needs a concluding section which summarizes the results.
- **Nature Physics (editorial "Elements of style", Nat. Phys. 3:581, 2007).** Conclusions are not mandatory; those that merely summarize the preceding results and discussion are unnecessary and will be edited out. A good conclusion offers something new, such as the implications of what the reader has just learned. This is the source of the test "could the reader have understood this sentence before reading the paper?".
- **Publisher templates.** A common guide for authors allows the main conclusions to be presented in a short Conclusions section, standing alone or as a subsection of the Discussion. IEEE's guidance for conference papers asks authors to summarize the key findings and include the conclusions that can be drawn and further implications for the field. PLOS ONE lets Discussion and Conclusions be separate or combined.

## Advice on repetition

- A short summarizing paragraph will do, and it should never simply repeat material from the abstract or introduction; statements with "investigated" or "studied" are not conclusions; most readers read the abstract and conclusion first (a research group's guide, TU Darmstadt).
- A conclusion is not a second discussion and not the abstract repeated at the end; if the discussion has done its job, two or three sentences are a legitimate length (CASRAI guide to writing a conclusion).
- Because the abstract provides much of the same information, many conclusions end up strongly resembling it (Hart 2020, "Reaching conclusions").
- Now that the reader has seen the results, the conclusion can give a more informed summary that contains insight ("A template and suggestions for writing easy-to-read research articles", arXiv:1907.12204). A university writing guide makes the same point: with the evidence presented, readers are ready for the strongest statement of the main argument.
- The first paragraph of the discussion summarizes the key findings, later paragraphs treat strengths, weaknesses and the literature, and the closing paragraph explains how the paper moves the field forward (Mensh & Kording 2017, PLOS Comput. Biol. 13:e1005619, Rule 8).

## How papers are read

- In the three-pass method of reading a paper, the first pass covers the title, abstract and introduction, the headings, and the conclusions, and the author notes that most reviewers and readers make only one pass (Keshav 2007, "How to read a paper", ACM SIGCOMM Comput. Commun. Rev. 37:83). This is why repetition among abstract, introduction and conclusion is what a first-pass reader sees, and why the part requires each to do a different job.

## The pattern found in published conclusions

- An analysis of research articles in applied linguistics found three moves in conclusion sections: summarizing the study; evaluating the study (its significance, its limitations, its method); and deductions from the research (recommending further research, drawing implications) (Yang & Allison 2003, English for Specific Purposes 22:365). The five moves of the part are these three, with the summary split into answer and basis, and the evaluation and deductions kept to one sentence each. The corpus is not from physics.

## Vague calls for more research

- "More research is needed" fits most papers, and some journals have banned the unqualified phrase; editors ask authors to say exactly what research is needed (BMJ 2006;333:804, "How to formulate research recommendations"). This is the basis for requiring a specific next step or none.

## This skill's own rules (no external source)

- "Same claim, different job, no shared sentence", and the table of what the abstract, introduction and conclusion each carry.
- Placing the scope sentence before the consequence so that the paper does not end on a caveat.
- The threshold of six shared words used by `overlap_check.py`, and the length guides (60 to 120 words in a letter, about 200 in a full article, about 5% of the main text).
- No pointers to figures, equations or sections in the conclusion.
