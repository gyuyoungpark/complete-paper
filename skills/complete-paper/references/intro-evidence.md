# Introduction: what the guidelines and studies say

Read this when you need to explain a choice made in `parts/intro.md`. Journal guidelines say little about how to build an introduction; they state who it is for and how long it may be. The structure comes from genre research and from editors' advice. None of it is a controlled test of what reads best. Say "this is the venue's requirement" or "this is the usual pattern", not "this is proven".

## What journals require

- **Nature.** Articles start with a fully referenced summary paragraph of ideally no more than 200 words. Nature's annotated example builds it as: one or two sentences of basic introduction for a scientist in any discipline; two or three sentences of more detailed background for related disciplines; one sentence stating the general problem; one sentence with the main result ("here we show"); then comparison with previous knowledge and general context. This is the broad, specific, problem, result sequence in miniature.
- **Nature-family research journals.** The main text of an Article begins with an introduction, without a heading, of referenced text that expands on the background of the work; some overlap with the abstract is acceptable (Nature Communications). A Nature Physics guide to authors gives up to 500 words for this introduction.
- **Science.** The abstract of a Research Article starts with a sentence of broad introduction comprehensible to the general reader and then a sentence of more detailed background specific to the study. The manuscript starts with a brief introduction describing the paper's significance. Research Articles are limited to 3000 words of main text. - **Physical Review Letters.** The introduction should give the general reader sufficient background and place the work in the context of the existing literature; Letters are written for a broad readership of physicists. Editors judge impact, innovation and interest.
- **Physical Review Applied.** Manuscripts should not include dramatic language or overstated claims of novelty or priority.

## The pattern found in published introductions

- **Create a Research Space (Swales 1990).** Analyses of introductions across disciplines find three moves: establishing a territory (claiming centrality, making topic generalizations, reviewing previous research); establishing a niche (indicating a gap, counter-claiming, raising a question, or continuing a tradition); occupying the niche (stating the purpose or the present research, announcing principal findings, indicating the structure of the article). The 2004 revision adds stating the value of the present research. The five moves of the part are these three, with the territory split into broad and specific interest and the value of filling the niche named as importance.
- **Announcing the findings.** In a sample of Physical Review, about 45% of introductions announced the principal findings, a share that had risen over the previous forty years; in the Journal of Educational Psychology it was under 7%. Most style manuals surveyed recommended announcing them (Swales & Najjar 1987, Written Communication 4:175). This is the basis for previewing the main finding by default in physics, and for checking the venue's habit elsewhere.

## Editors' and authors' advice

- **Progressively narrower paragraphs.** Go from the field-level gap to the specific gap the paper fills; avoid a broad literature review beyond what motivates the work; the final paragraph briefly previews the results, with less context and somewhat more specificity than the abstract (Mensh & Kording 2017, PLOS Comput. Biol. 13:e1005619, Rule 6).
- **Just enough background.** Provide just enough background for the reader to understand how the question fills a gap in the knowledge of the field (editorial "Scientific writing 101", Nat. Struct. Mol. Biol. 17:139, 2010).
- **The opening paragraph carries the paper.** It should tell the central story and why it matters, set the scene for non-specialists, and avoid diving into technical detail or listing what was done; clichés and hollow generalities should give way to specific open problems (editorial "Elements of style", Nat. Phys. 3:581, 2007).
- **Frame, do not overclaim.** The introduction puts the work in the context of the literature and engages the reader's interest (Nature Methods editorial, doi:10.1038/nmeth.4532).
- **Contributions drive the paper.** In computing, the list of contributions is written first and the rest of the paper substantiates it (Peyton Jones, "How to write a great research paper").

## This skill's own rules (no external source)

- Splitting "importance" into the importance of the field (move 1) and the importance of closing the gap (move 4).
- The test that an opening sentence must not be able to open hundreds of other papers.
- Stating the gap as where existing work stops, in preference to an absolute negative.
- Citing only works seen in the session or supplied by the user, with visible placeholders otherwise, and recording a literature check for the gap. These follow from the risk of fabricated references in machine-assisted writing, not from a journal rule.
- The paragraph shapes given for letters and full articles.
