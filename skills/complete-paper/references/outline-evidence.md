# Outline: what the sources say and what is synthesis

Read this when you need to explain a choice made in `parts/outline.md`. The sources are advice from experienced authors and editors, most of them in the life sciences and chemistry. None is a controlled study, and none prescribes what each numbered figure must contain. Say "this is established advice" or "this is this skill's synthesis", not "this is proven".

Checked 2026-11-19 from the publishers' pages. Verify before citing elsewhere.

## Advice the sources agree on

- **One central contribution.** A paper should focus on a single message communicated in the title; a multifaceted paper can work if a clear bridge links its facets (Mensh & Kording 2017, PLOS Comput. Biol. 13:e1005619, Rule 1). A paper should have one clear idea, and the list of contributions drives the whole paper (Peyton Jones, "How to write a great research paper").
- **Outline before text, around the data.** An outline is an organized set of data with objectives, hypotheses and conclusions, containing little text; several cycles of outline come before any writing; do not wait until the data are complete (Whitesides 2004, Adv. Mater. 16:1375). Outline with one informal sentence per planned paragraph, each paragraph with a defined role (Mensh & Kording, Rule 9).
- **Statements, not topics.** Turn the steps of the argument into declarative subsection headers and figure titles; a figure title communicates the conclusion and the legend explains how the analysis was done (Mensh & Kording, Rule 7). Whitesides gives the same advice for headings, preferring a heading that states the finding to "Measurement of Rates".
- **Logic, not chronology.** Readers care about the claim and the logic supporting it, not the path to it (Mensh & Kording, Rule 3). The order of presentation can and often should differ from the order of execution; a paper is not an autobiography (Sanes 2019, eLife 8:e50527).
- **One place per subject.** Avoid zig-zag; express parallel ideas in parallel form (Mensh & Kording, Rule 4).
- **One point per figure, and few figures.** Each figure should make just one point and be self-explanatory; too many display items dilute the message; include a figure only when it is needed to support a key conclusion (editorial "Go figure", Nat. Struct. Mol. Biol. 17:1163, 2010).
- **Figures readable on their own.** An expert should understand a figure's main information without consulting the main text; technical detail essential to the science but not to the narrative goes to Methods or supplementary information (editorial "Elements of style", Nat. Phys. 3:581, 2007).
- **A closing model figure.** A figure illustrating the final model conveys the big picture and "has resonance with the abstract"; a model placed at the start needs care (the same NSMB editorial).
- **Test by telling.** If the outline cannot be described to a colleague in a few minutes, readers will not follow it either (Mensh & Kording, Rule 10).
- **Conclusions must add something.** A conclusion that merely summarizes is unnecessary (Nat. Phys. editorial).

## Where the sources disagree

| Question | One view | Other view | What the part does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Figures first or text first? | Whitesides: organize the data and sketch the figures in the outline, then write | Sanes: start with the Introduction and Results, leave the figures to later, because panels are always rearranged | Claim sentences, then figure specifications, then text; figures are polished after the Results draft |
| Model figure first or last? | NSMB: usually last | NSMB also notes some papers open with it | Synthesize is suggested for the last item; an opening model must not overstate |

## Venue arithmetic used as the example

APS length guide: total word count is text plus displayed mathematics plus figures plus tables. A single-column figure counts (150 / aspect ratio) + 20 words; a double-column figure counts 300 / (0.5 x aspect ratio) + 40 words; displayed mathematics counts 16 words per single-column row. A Letter in Physical Review Letters is limited to 3750 words. Aspect ratio is width over height, so the cost of a double-column figure ranges from 340 words (twice as wide as tall) to 640 words (square).

## This skill's synthesis (no external source)

- The role vocabulary (Orient, Establish, Discriminate, Generalize, Benchmark, Synthesize, Limit) and the rule of exactly one Establish item within the first two display items.
- The table of starting patterns by type of paper.
- "Three to six" statements in the claim chain. The NSMB editorial reports six or seven display items as typical for its papers; letters in physics usually carry fewer.
- The deletion test as the criterion for moving an item to the supplement, and the order of cuts under a length limit.
- The default split of words between sections.
- The preference for a Discriminate item over additional agreeing examples.

## From the author's working notes, not re-verified here

- That overview figures are the first to be cut under space pressure and should be designed compactly (attributed to a Nature Methods column).
- That a method's own failure cases deserve a figure type of their own (attributed to a SIGGRAPH writing guide); this is the origin of the Limit role.
- That advice in machine learning favours an eye-catching first-page figure, while another guide warns that leading with the single best result raises expectations the paper may not meet.
- That a key result named in the abstract but absent from every figure is an omission (attributed to a research group's guide).
- That one guide to writing Letters places the main text last, after figures and captions are complete.

Treat these as leads. Check the original before repeating any of them as a source.
